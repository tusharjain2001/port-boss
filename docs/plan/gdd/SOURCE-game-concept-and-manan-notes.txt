

🎮 Game Concept: PORT — Rise of an Empire 12 Visual direction: humble fishing village evolving into a massive commercial port. Genre: Co-op, open-world, business simulation, adventure, light survival Players: 1–4 (my suggested starting target is 2-player co-op) Main goal: Go from a poor fisherman to the owner of the most successful shipping and trading empire in the region. 1. The main story Chapter 1 — The Fisherman The game begins in a small coastal town called Greyhaven. The player is a struggling fisherman living in a tiny wooden house near the ocean. He owns only three things: an old fishing boat, a rusty fishing net, and a small dock that barely fits his boat. Every morning, he watches enormous cargo ships entering the city's commercial port. All of these ships belong to Adrian Voss, the wealthiest businessman in the region. Adrian Voss — The Rival Voss started as a fisherman himself, but over the years he acquired most of the region's warehouses, shipping contracts, fishing fleets, and transport businesses. He's respected by the wealthy, feared by smaller traders, and quietly controls much of the port's economy. At the beginning, the player has to sell fish to businesses controlled by Voss because there are very few other buyers. One morning, the player catches a particularly valuable fish. He arrives at the market, but Voss's purchasing agent offers him an extremely low price. The player refuses. The agent replies: "You can sell it here, or take it home and watch it rot. There's no other buyer in this town." This is the moment that introduces the main conflict. The player decides to build his own business rather than work under Voss forever. First objective: Earn enough money to repair the boat and find an independent buyer. Chapter 2 — The First Real Business The player discovers an independent restaurant on another island whose owner pays much more for fresh fish. But there's a problem. The restaurant is far away, the player's boat has limited fuel, and fish lose freshness during transportation. So now players need to plan their trips. They can take smaller loads to the nearby market for less money or risk traveling farther for a more profitable order. Gradually, they earn enough to buy a better motor, larger fishing nets, storage boxes, and their first small warehouse. Eventually, the player receives a major order from a hotel. Voss notices that one of his regular customers has stopped buying from him. For the first time, the rival knows the player exists. Chapter 3 — The Rival Strikes Back As the player's business grows, Voss starts interfering. He offers discounts to the player's customers, buys exclusive rights to valuable fishing areas, blocks access to some docks, and uses his influence over other traders. Later, things become more dangerous. One night, the player returns to find his boat damaged. A trusted local mechanic believes somebody deliberately sabotaged it. The player must investigate, gather evidence, and find new ways to protect the business. The player's goal now shifts from making money to building an independent transport and trading network that Voss cannot control. Chapter 4 — Building the Empire 12 Players begin acquiring businesses beyond fishing. They can open a fish-processing factory, operate delivery trucks, hire crews, expand into cargo transportation, build additional docks, and eventually purchase commercial vessels. This is where the game becomes more than just fishing. Imagine starting with a wooden boat carrying 20 fish and eventually owning a massive vessel transporting hundreds of cargo containers. Voss starts losing his biggest contracts. He now sees the player as his greatest competitor. Chapter 5 — The Port War A major contract becomes available to operate a newly built international port terminal. Both companies compete for it. Voss has more money, more connections, and a much bigger fleet. But the player has something Voss has lost: the trust of local businesses and independent traders. The final chapter involves securing multiple supply chains, fulfilling enormous cargo contracts, surviving sabotage, and bringing competing businesses together. At the climax, a major storm strikes the port. Both companies' ships are threatened, including one carrying vital emergency supplies. The player can prioritize the lucrative final shipment or temporarily divert their fleet to rescue stranded sailors and protect the town. Their actions affect which businesses support them in the final competition. The ending: Players can finally overtake Voss and become the dominant port operator. Depending on their decisions, they can either continue Voss's aggressive business model or build a trading network where smaller businesses can thrive alongside them. 2. The most important thing: the gameplay loop We need something players genuinely enjoy repeating, even without progressing the story. Catch fish Store cargo Transport Sell / Deliver Earn money Upgrade Each cycle earns money, unlocks bigger jobs, and creates new opportunities. The fun should come from making decisions, operating equipment, completing orders together, and watching the business physically grow. 3. Should the first shop be far away? My answer: Yes, there should be a distant shop — but not as the only shop. I'd design the starting map like this: Illustrative starter map — not to scale. Routes and locations unlock as the players progress. Here's how I would balance it: Destination Travel time Benefit Small village market 30–45 seconds Very low prices, easy selling Nearby town market 1–2 minutes Average prices and regular orders Premium island restaurant 3–4 minutes High-paying orders and rare fish Voss's commercial port Locked initially Major contracts and trading later These are suggested gameplay targets, not realistic travel times. The player should struggle because of limited resources and tough choices, not because traveling takes 15 minutes every time they want to sell something. 4. Features and progression Stage 1 — Poor fisherman Small wooden boat, manual fishing, limited storage, high fuel costs, basic orders, and repairing damaged equipment. Players manually unload their catch and carry boxes to the market. Stage 2 — Small trader Motorboat, fishing nets, ice storage, bigger contracts, a delivery trolley, and the first small warehouse. Players can accept timed restaurant orders and buy a small pickup truck. Stage 3 — Business owner Medium trawlers, hired workers, trucks, refrigerated storage, processing factories, and multiple shops. Players begin managing fishing fleets and delivering goods by road and sea. Stage 4 — Shipping company Cargo ships, containers, cranes, large warehouses, international shipping contracts, and multiple operating ports. Players can diversify into transporting machinery, furniture, food, and construction materials. Stage 5 — Port tycoon Own terminals, buy shipping companies, operate entire fleets, manage trade routes, compete for international contracts, and challenge Voss for control of the port. 5. The co-op mechanics must be special This is probably the most important part of your game, because it's meant to be played together. Imagine two friends operating the same fishing boat. gamepro.de Player 1 is driving the boat, watching fuel, avoiding rocks, and finding fishing spots. Player 2 is operating fishing nets, pulling fish out of the sea, sorting the catch, and fixing equipment. Suddenly, a storm starts. Player 1 needs to control the boat while Player 2 secures cargo and repairs a damaged engine. Or later, when both have their own vehicles: One player can sail to a distant island to pick up valuable cargo, while the other drives a delivery truck to a different city. Both players are contributing to the same business. The co-op features I would prioritize are: - Shared business account: Both players earn money for their company. - Flexible roles: Anyone can fish, drive, trade, load cargo, or manage the business. - Physical cargo handling: Carry crates, operate cranes, and load vehicles together. - Emergency teamwork: Storms, engine breakdowns, and damaged cargo create memorable moments. - Multiple vehicles: Eventually, friends can work independently and coordinate deliveries. - Shared progression: Everyone sees the company's docks, warehouse, and fleet improve. 6. Rival behavior should evolve with your success Voss shouldn't simply appear in cutscenes. His company should actively compete with the player. Your company level Rival's reaction Beginner Ignores you Small trader Offers better prices to your buyers Growing business Tries to take over your contracts Established company Interferes with routes, docks, and supply chains Major competitor Starts aggressive commercial competition and covert sabotage Empire level Challenges you for the region's biggest contracts Players need ways to counter his attacks, such as building relationships, securing alternative routes, hiring security, negotiating, and investing in better infrastructure. Importantly, the rival shouldn't randomly destroy everything the player spent hours building. Rival actions should create challenging, recoverable situations, with warnings and counterplay. 7. Features that make the world feel alive I would add NPCs with routines and business needs, a day-night cycle, dynamic weather, different fish species, changing commodity prices, rare cargo opportunities, repair shops, an active harbor workforce, and seasonal events. But not all at once. The game needs a small, satisfying core before expanding into a complicated tycoon simulation. 8. What the first 30 minutes could look like 1. 0–5 minutes: The introduction Players arrive at the old dock, see Voss's massive ship, receive a simple fishing tutorial, and take their first boat out to sea. 2. 5–12 minutes: The first catch Players catch fish together, return to the harbor, unload their crates, and discover how little the local buyer is willing to pay. 3. 12–18 minutes: A better opportunity A restaurant owner calls offering much better prices, but the delivery requires a longer sea journey and freshness management. 4. 18–25 minutes: The first risky delivery Players coordinate navigation, fuel, cargo loading, and delivery. They earn their first meaningful profit. 5. 25–30 minutes: The first upgrade They buy a better engine or larger storage. A short scene shows Voss's agent noticing that an independent trader has fulfilled a valuable order. The first 30 minutes should end with the feeling that the players have made actual progress, but there's a much bigger world ahead of them. My biggest suggestion One thing I'd strongly recommend is making the entire game world change as the business grows. At the beginning, the player lives next to a broken wooden dock. By the middle, there are workers loading cargo, trucks departing warehouses, and boats returning from fishing trips. By the end, the player can stand on their office balcony and see dozens of their own ships operating throughout the harbor. That's the kind of progression people love in business-building games.
